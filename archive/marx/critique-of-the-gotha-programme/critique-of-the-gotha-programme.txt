CRITIQUE OF THE GOTHA PROGRAMME

by Karl Marx

Translated from the German for the TIA archive, 2026

FOREWORD BY FREDERICK ENGELS (1891)

The manuscript printed here — the covering letter to Bracke as well as
the critique of the draft programme — was sent to Bracke in 1875,
shortly before the Gotha unity congress, to be passed on to Geib, Auer,
Bebel and Liebknecht and afterwards returned to Marx. Since the Halle
party congress has put the discussion of the Gotha Programme on the
party's agenda, I believe I would be committing an act of suppression if
I withheld from the public any longer this important — perhaps the most
important — document bearing on that discussion.

But the manuscript has another and more far-reaching significance. Here,
for the first time, Marx's attitude to the course Lassalle had taken
from the moment he entered the agitation is set out clearly and firmly,
both as regards Lassalle's economic principles and as regards his
tactics.

The ruthless sharpness with which the draft programme is dissected here,
the relentlessness with which the conclusions are stated and the weak
points of the draft laid bare — none of this can give offence any
longer, today, after fifteen years. Specific Lassalleans now survive
only abroad, as isolated ruins, and at Halle the Gotha Programme was
given up even by its own authors as thoroughly inadequate.

Nevertheless, in a few places where it made no difference to the
substance, I have left out some sharp personal expressions and
judgements and replaced them with dots. Marx himself would do the same
if he were publishing the manuscript today. The language, violent in
places, was provoked by two circumstances. In the first place, Marx and
I were more closely bound up with the German movement than with any
other; the decided step backwards shown in this draft programme was
therefore bound to move us especially strongly. In the second place, at
that time, barely two years after the Hague congress of the
International, we were in the thick of our fight with Bakunin and his
anarchists, who held us responsible for everything that happened in the
labour movement in Germany; we therefore had to expect that the secret
paternity of this programme, too, would be laid at our door. These
considerations no longer apply, and with them goes the need for the
passages in question.

A few sentences have also been indicated only by dots, for reasons
connected with the press laws. Where I had to choose a milder
expression, it is placed in square brackets. Otherwise the text is
reproduced word for word.

                        London, 6 January 1891

                        Frederick Engels

_Engels cut a handful of passages when he first published these notes in
1891, as he explains above. This translation follows Marx's own
manuscript, so those passages are included here in full and there are no
dots or square brackets._

LETTER TO WILHELM BRACKE

_London, 5 May 1875_

Dear Bracke,

When you have read the following critical marginal notes on the
coalition programme, would you be so good as to pass them on for
inspection to Geib and Auer, Bebel and Liebknecht. I am overloaded with
work and already have to go far beyond the amount of work my doctor
allows me. So it was by no means a “pleasure” to write such a long
screed. It was necessary, however, so that the party friends for whom
this communication is intended will not later misread the steps I shall
have to take.

For after the unity congress has been held, Engels and I will publish a
short statement to the effect that we stand entirely aloof from the said
programme of principles and have nothing to do with it.

This is indispensable, because abroad the view is held — a view
carefully fostered by the party's enemies, and a completely mistaken one
— that we secretly direct the movement of the so-called Eisenach party
from here. In a Russian pamphlet that appeared only recently, for
example, Bakunin still holds me responsible not only for all the
programmes, etc., of that party, but even for every step Liebknecht has
taken since the day he began co-operating with the People's Party.

Apart from this, it is my duty not to give my recognition, even by
diplomatic silence, to a programme which in my conviction is thoroughly
objectionable and demoralising for the party.

Every step of real movement is more important than a dozen programmes.
If, therefore, it was not possible — and the circumstances of the time
did not permit it — to go _beyond_ the Eisenach programme, one should
simply have concluded an agreement for action against the common enemy.
But when one draws up programmes of principles (instead of putting them
off until a time when such things have been prepared by a longer period
of common activity), one sets up before the whole world landmarks by
which it measures how far the party movement has got.

The leaders of the Lassalleans came because circumstances forced them
to. Had they been told from the outset that there would be no haggling
over principles, they would _have had_ to content themselves with a
programme of action or a plan of organisation for common action. Instead
of this, they are allowed to arrive armed with mandates, and these
mandates are recognised on our side as binding, which means surrendering
unconditionally to people who are themselves in need of help. To crown
it all, they are holding a congress of their own _before_ the _congress
of compromise_, while our own party is holding its congress _post
festum_. We know how the mere fact of unification pleases the workers,
but it is a mistake to believe that this momentary success has not been
bought too dearly. In any case the programme is worthless, even apart
from its canonisation of the Lassallean articles of faith.

I shall be sending you the last instalments of the French edition of
_Capital_ shortly. The progress of the printing was held up for a long
time by a ban imposed by the French government. The thing will be
finished this week or at the beginning of next. Did you receive the
previous six instalments? Please also let me have the _address_ of
Bernhard Becker, to whom I must likewise send the last instalments.

The _Volksstaat_ bookshop has peculiar manners. Up to this very moment,
for example, I have not been sent a single copy of the reprint of the
_Cologne Communist Trial_.

With best regards,

                        Yours, Karl Marx

MARGINAL NOTES ON THE PROGRAMME OF THE GERMAN WORKERS' PARTY

I

    1. “Labour is the source of all wealth and all culture, _and
    since_ useful labour is possible only in society and through
    society, the proceeds of labour belong undiminished, with equal
    right, to all members of society.”

_First part of the paragraph:_ “Labour is the source of all wealth and
all culture.”

Labour is _not the source_ of all wealth. _Nature_ is just as much the
source of use-values (and it is surely of these that material wealth
consists!) as labour, which is itself only the expression of a force of
nature, human labour-power. That phrase is to be found in every
children's primer, and it is correct in so far as it is _taken for
granted_ that labour is carried on with the objects and instruments that
go with it. But a socialist programme cannot allow such bourgeois turns
of phrase to pass over in silence the _conditions_ that alone give them
meaning. Only in so far as man from the very start behaves towards
nature, the first source of all instruments and objects of labour, as
its owner, and treats it as belonging to him, does his labour become a
source of use-values, and therefore also of wealth. The bourgeois have
very good reasons for crediting labour with _supernatural creative
power_; for it follows precisely from the fact that labour is
conditioned by nature that the man who possesses no other property than
his labour-power must, in every condition of society and culture, be the
slave of the other men who have made themselves the owners of the
material conditions of labour. He can work only with their permission,
and so can live only with their permission.

Let us now leave the sentence as it stands, or rather limps. What would
one have expected as its conclusion? Obviously this:

    “Since labour is the source of all wealth, no one in society can
    appropriate wealth except as the product of labour. If, therefore,
    he does not work himself, he lives on the labour of others and
    also acquires his culture at the expense of the labour of others.”

Instead of this, a second sentence is tacked on by means of the verbal
screw “and since”, in order to draw a conclusion from it and not from
the first.

_Second part of the paragraph:_ “Useful labour is possible only in
society and through society.”

According to the first sentence, labour was the source of all wealth and
all culture, and so no society was possible without labour either. Now
we learn, the other way round, that no “useful” labour is possible
without society.

One could just as well have said that only in society can useless and
even socially harmful labour become a way of earning a living, that only
in society can one live by idleness, etc., etc. — in short, one could
have copied out the whole of Rousseau.

And what is “useful” labour? Surely only labour that produces the useful
effect intended. A savage — and man is a savage once he has ceased to be
an ape — who kills an animal with a stone, who gathers fruit, etc.,
performs “useful” labour.

_Thirdly, the conclusion:_ “And since useful labour is possible only in
society and through society, the proceeds of labour belong undiminished,
with equal right, to all members of society.”

A fine conclusion! If useful labour is possible only in society and
through society, then the proceeds of labour belong to society — and
only so much of them comes to the individual worker as is not needed to
maintain the “condition” of labour, society.

In fact this proposition has been put forward at all times _by the
champions of whatever state of society existed at the time_. First come
the claims of the government and everything that clings to it, since it
is the social organ for maintaining social order; then come the claims
of the various kinds of private owners, since the various kinds of
private property are the foundations of society, and so on. One sees
that such hollow phrases can be twisted and turned any way one likes.

The first and second parts of the paragraph have some intelligible
connection only in this wording:

“Labour becomes the source of wealth and culture only as social labour”,
or, what is the same thing, “in and through society”. This proposition
is indisputably correct, for although isolated labour (its material
conditions being presupposed) can create use-values, it can create
neither wealth nor culture.

But the other proposition is just as indisputable:

    “In proportion as labour develops socially, and thereby becomes
    the source of wealth and culture, poverty and neglect develop on
    the side of the worker, and wealth and culture on the side of the
    non-worker.”

This is the law of all history up to now. What had to be done here,
then, instead of making general phrases about “_labour_” and
“_society_”, was to show concretely how in present-day capitalist
society the material and other conditions have at last been created
which enable the workers, and compel them, to break that historical
curse.

In fact, however, the whole paragraph, botched in style and in content,
is there only so as to inscribe the Lassallean catchword of the
“undiminished proceeds of labour” as a slogan at the top of the party
banner. I shall come back later to the “proceeds of labour”, “equal
right”, etc., since the same thing comes up again in a somewhat
different form.

    2. “In present-day society the means of labour are the monopoly of
    the capitalist class; the resulting dependence of the working
    class is the cause of misery and servitude in all its forms.”

This sentence, borrowed from the Rules of the International, is wrong in
this “improved” edition.

In present-day society the means of labour are the monopoly of the
landowners (the monopoly of landed property is even the basis of the
monopoly of capital) _and_ of the capitalists. In the passage in
question, the Rules of the International name neither the one class of
monopolists nor the other. They speak of the “_monopoly of the means of
labour, that is, of the sources of life_”; the addition “sources of
life” shows clearly enough that the land is included among the means of
labour.

The improvement was introduced because Lassalle, for reasons now
generally known, attacked _only_ the capitalist class, not the
landowners. In England the capitalist is usually not even the owner of
the land on which his factory stands.

    3. “The emancipation of labour demands the elevation of the means
    of labour to the common property of society and the co-operative
    regulation of the total labour, with a just distribution of the
    proceeds of labour.”

“Elevation of the means of labour to common property”! This is
presumably meant to say their “conversion into common property”. But
that only in passing.

What are the “_proceeds of labour_”? The product of labour, or its
value? And in the latter case, the total value of the product, or only
that part of the value which labour has newly added to the value of the
means of production used up?

“Proceeds of labour” is a loose notion that Lassalle has put in the
place of definite economic concepts.

What is “just” distribution?

Do not the bourgeois assert that present-day distribution is “just”? And
is it not, in fact, the only “just” distribution on the basis of the
present-day mode of production? Are economic relations regulated by
legal concepts, or do not, on the contrary, legal relations arise out of
economic ones? Have not the socialist sectarians, too, the most varied
notions about “just” distribution?

To find out what is to be understood on this occasion by the phrase
“just distribution”, we must take the first paragraph together with this
one. The latter presupposes a society in which “the means of labour are
common property and the total labour is co-operatively regulated”, and
from the first paragraph we learn that “the proceeds of labour belong
undiminished, with equal right, to all members of society”. “To all
members of society”? To those who do not work as well? What then becomes
of the “undiminished proceeds of labour”? Only to the members of society
who work? What then becomes of the “equal right” of all members of
society?

But “all members of society” and “equal right” are obviously mere
phrases. The heart of the matter is that in this communist society every
worker is supposed to receive his “undiminished” Lassallean “proceeds of
labour”.

If we first take the words “proceeds of labour” in the sense of the
product of labour, then the co-operative proceeds of labour are the
_total social product_.

From this there must now be deducted:

_First_, cover for the replacement of the _means of production_ used up.

_Secondly_, an additional portion for the expansion of production.

_Thirdly_, a reserve or insurance fund against accidents, disruptions
caused by natural events, etc.

These deductions from the “undiminished proceeds of labour” are an
economic necessity, and their size is to be determined according to the
means and forces available, partly by the calculation of probabilities,
but they can in no way be calculated from justice.

There remains the other part of the total product, intended to serve as
means of consumption. Before this comes to be divided among individuals,
there is again deducted from it:

_First_, the general costs of administration not directly belonging to
production.

This part will from the very outset be very considerably restricted in
comparison with present-day society, and it diminishes in proportion as
the new society develops.

_Secondly_, what is intended for the common satisfaction of needs, such
as schools, health services, etc.

This part grows considerably from the very outset in comparison with
present-day society, and it increases in proportion as the new society
develops.

_Thirdly_, funds for those unable to work, etc., in short, for what
today comes under so-called official poor relief.

Only now do we come to the “distribution” which the programme, under
Lassallean influence, has narrow-mindedly in view to the exclusion of
everything else, namely to that part of the means of consumption which
is divided among the individual producers of the co-operative.

The “undiminished proceeds of labour” have meanwhile already turned on
the quiet into “diminished” proceeds, although what the producer is
deprived of in his capacity as a private individual benefits him,
directly or indirectly, in his capacity as a member of society.

Just as the phrase “undiminished proceeds of labour” has disappeared, so
now the phrase “proceeds of labour” disappears altogether.

Within the co-operative society based on common ownership of the means
of production, the producers do not exchange their products; just as
little does the labour spent on the products appear here as the _value_
of these products, as a material property they possess, since now, in
contrast to capitalist society, individual pieces of labour no longer
exist as component parts of the total labour by a roundabout route, but
directly. The words “proceeds of labour”, objectionable even today on
account of their ambiguity, thus lose all meaning.

What we are dealing with here is a communist society, not as it has
_developed_ on its own foundations, but, on the contrary, just as it
_emerges_ from capitalist society; a society which is thus in every
respect, economically, morally and intellectually, still marked with the
birthmarks of the old society from whose womb it comes. Accordingly, the
individual producer receives back from society — after the deductions —
exactly what he gives to it. What he has given to it is his individual
quantity of labour. For example, the social working day consists of the
sum of the individual hours of work. The individual labour time of the
individual producer is the part of the social working day that he has
contributed, his share of it. He receives a certificate from society
stating that he has furnished such and such an amount of labour (after
deduction of his labour for the common funds), and with this certificate
he draws from the social stock of means of consumption as much as costs
the same amount of labour. The same quantity of labour that he has given
to society in one form he receives back in another.

Here, obviously, the same principle prevails as that which regulates the
exchange of commodities, in so far as this is an exchange of equal
values. Content and form are changed, because under the changed
circumstances no one can give anything except his labour, and because,
on the other hand, nothing can pass into the ownership of individuals
except individual means of consumption. But as far as the distribution
of the latter among the individual producers is concerned, the same
principle prevails as in the exchange of commodity equivalents: a given
amount of labour in one form is exchanged for an equal amount of labour
in another form.

_Equal right_ here is therefore still — in principle — _bourgeois
right_, although principle and practice are no longer at each other's
throats, whereas in the exchange of commodities the exchange of
equivalents exists only _on the average_ and not in the individual case.

In spite of this advance, this _equal right_ is still constantly
burdened with a bourgeois limitation. The right of the producers is
_proportional_ to the labour they supply; the equality consists in the
fact that measurement is made with an _equal standard_, labour. But one
man is superior to another physically or mentally, and so supplies more
labour in the same time, or can work for a longer time; and labour, to
serve as a measure, must be defined by its duration or its intensity,
otherwise it ceases to be a standard of measurement. This _equal_ right
is unequal right for unequal labour. It recognises no class differences,
because everyone is only a worker like everyone else; but it tacitly
recognises unequal individual endowment, and thus unequal productive
capacity of the workers, as natural privileges. _It is therefore a right
of inequality, in its content, like every right._ Right by its very
nature can consist only in the application of an equal standard; but
unequal individuals (and they would not be different individuals if they
were not unequal) can be measured by an equal standard only in so far as
they are brought under an equal point of view, are taken from one
_particular_ side only — for instance, in the present case, are regarded
_only as workers_ and nothing more is seen in them, everything else
being disregarded. Further: one worker is married, another is not; one
has more children than another, and so on and so forth. Thus, with an
equal performance of labour, and hence an equal share in the social
consumption fund, one will in fact receive more than another, one will
be richer than another, and so on. To avoid all these defects, right
would have to be unequal rather than equal.

But these defects are unavoidable in the first phase of communist
society, as it has just emerged from capitalist society after long birth
pangs. Right can never be higher than the economic structure of society
and the cultural development conditioned by it.

In a higher phase of communist society, after the enslaving
subordination of individuals to the division of labour has vanished, and
with it the antithesis between mental and physical labour; after labour
has become not only a means of life but itself the first need of life;
after the productive forces have also grown with the all-round
development of individuals, and all the springs of co-operative wealth
flow more abundantly — only then can the narrow horizon of bourgeois
right be left behind entirely, and society inscribe on its banners: From
each according to his abilities, to each according to his needs!

I have dealt at greater length with the “undiminished proceeds of
labour” on the one hand, and with “equal right” and “just distribution”
on the other, in order to show what an offence it is, on the one hand,
to try to force on our party once more, as dogmas, ideas which made
sense at a certain time but have now become obsolete verbal rubbish,
and, on the other hand, to twist the realistic outlook, which it cost so
much effort to instil into the party but which has now taken root in it,
with ideological nonsense about right and other trash of the kind so
familiar to the democrats and the French socialists.

Quite apart from what has been set out so far, it was in general a
mistake to make a fuss about so-called _distribution_ and to put the
main emphasis on it.

Any distribution whatever of the means of consumption is only a
consequence of the distribution of the conditions of production
themselves. The capitalist mode of production, for example, rests on the
fact that the material conditions of production are in the hands of
non-workers in the form of property in capital and property in land,
while the masses are owners only of the personal condition of
production, labour-power. If the elements of production are distributed
in this way, then the present-day distribution of the means of
consumption follows of its own accord. If the material conditions of
production are the co-operative property of the workers themselves, then
a distribution of the means of consumption different from the present
one follows in just the same way. Vulgar socialism (and from it in turn
a section of the democrats) has taken over from the bourgeois economists
the practice of considering and treating distribution as independent of
the mode of production, and hence of presenting socialism as turning
principally on distribution. Now that the real relation has long since
been made clear, why go backwards again?

    4. “The emancipation of labour must be the work of the working
    class, in relation to which all other classes are _only one
    reactionary mass_.”

The first strophe is taken from the opening words of the Rules of the
International, but “improved”. There it says: “The emancipation of the
working class must be the act of the workers themselves”; here, on the
other hand, “the working class” has to emancipate — what? “Labour”. Let
him understand who can.

By way of compensation, the antistrophe is a Lassallean quotation of the
purest water: “in relation to which (the working class) all other
classes form _only one reactionary mass_”.

In the _Communist Manifesto_ it says: “Of all the classes that stand
opposed to the bourgeoisie today, the proletariat alone is a _really
revolutionary class_. The other classes decay and go under with
large-scale industry; the proletariat is its most characteristic
product.”

The bourgeoisie is conceived here as a revolutionary class — as the
bearer of large-scale industry — in relation to the feudal lords and the
middle estates, who want to hold on to all the social positions that are
the creation of obsolete modes of production. They do not, therefore,
form _together with the bourgeoisie_ only one reactionary mass.

On the other hand, the proletariat is revolutionary in relation to the
bourgeoisie because, having itself grown up on the ground of large-scale
industry, it strives to strip production of the capitalist character
that the bourgeoisie seeks to perpetuate. But the _Manifesto_ adds that
the “middle estates … (become) revolutionary … in view of their
impending transfer into the proletariat”.

From this point of view, therefore, it is once again nonsense to say
that they, “together with the bourgeoisie”, and with the feudal lords
into the bargain, “form only one reactionary mass” in relation to the
working class.

At the last elections, did anyone call out to the artisans, small
manufacturers, etc., and _peasants_: “In relation to us you, together
with the bourgeois and the feudal lords, form only one reactionary
mass”?

Lassalle knew the _Communist Manifesto_ by heart, as his faithful know
the sacred writings of which he is the author. If he falsified it so
grossly, then, it was only in order to put a good face on his alliance
with the absolutist and feudal opponents against the bourgeoisie.

In the above paragraph, moreover, his oracular saying is dragged in by
the hair, without any connection at all with the botched quotation from
the Rules of the International. So here it is simply an impertinence,
and one by no means displeasing to Herr Bismarck, one of those cheap
pieces of loutishness that the Marat of Berlin goes in for.

    5. “The working class strives for its emancipation first of all
    _within the framework of the present-day national state_,
    conscious that the necessary result of its efforts, which are
    common to the workers of all civilised countries, will be the
    international brotherhood of peoples.”

Lassalle, in contrast to the _Communist Manifesto_ and to all earlier
socialism, had conceived the workers' movement from the narrowest
national standpoint. He is being followed in this — and that after the
work of the International!

It goes without saying that, in order to be able to fight at all, the
working class must organise itself at home as a class, and that its own
country is the immediate arena of its struggle. To that extent its class
struggle is national, not in its content but, as the _Communist
Manifesto_ says, “in its form”. But the “framework of the present-day
national state”, for instance the German Empire, itself stands in its
turn economically “within the framework of the world market” and
politically “within the framework of the system of states”. Any merchant
you care to name knows that German trade is at the same time foreign
trade, and the greatness of Herr Bismarck consists precisely in his own
kind of _international_ policy.

And to what does the German workers' party reduce its internationalism?
To the consciousness that the result of its efforts “will be _the
international brotherhood of peoples_” — a phrase borrowed from the
bourgeois League of Peace and Freedom, which is meant to pass as an
equivalent for the international brotherhood of the working classes in
their common struggle against the ruling classes and their governments.
So not a word about the _international functions_ of the German working
class! And this is how it is supposed to stand up to its own bourgeoisie
— which is already linked in brotherhood against it with the bourgeois
of all other countries — and to Herr Bismarck's international policy of
conspiracy!

In fact, the internationalism professed by the programme stands _even
infinitely below_ that of the Free Trade party. That party too asserts
that the result of its efforts will be “the international brotherhood of
peoples”. But it also _does_ something to make trade international, and
by no means contents itself with the consciousness that all peoples
carry on trade at home.

The international activity of the working classes does not in any way
depend on the existence of the _International Working Men's
Association_. That was only the first attempt to create a central organ
for this activity; an attempt which, through the impetus it gave, has
had a lasting success, but which in _its first historical form_ could no
longer be carried on after the fall of the Paris Commune.

Bismarck's _Norddeutsche_ was entirely right when it announced, to its
master's satisfaction, that in its new programme the German workers'
party had forsworn internationalism.

II

    “Starting from these principles, the German workers' party strives
    by all legal means for the _free state_ — _and_ — socialist
    society; the abolition of the wage system _together with_ the
    _iron law of wages_ — and — exploitation in every form; the
    elimination of all social and political inequality.”

I shall come back to the “free state” later.

So, in future, the German workers' party has got to believe in
Lassalle's “iron law of wages”! So that it shall not be lost, the
nonsense is committed of speaking of the “abolition of the wage system”
(it should read: the system of wage labour) “_together with_ the iron
law of wages”. If I abolish wage labour, then naturally I abolish its
laws as well, whether they are “iron” or spongy. But Lassalle's attack
on wage labour turns almost entirely on this so-called law. To prove,
therefore, that the Lassallean sect has won, the “wage system” must be
abolished “_together with_ the iron law of wages” and not without it.

Of the “iron law of wages”, as is well known, nothing belongs to
Lassalle except the word “iron”, borrowed from Goethe's “eternal, iron,
great laws”. The word _iron_ is a label by which the true believers
recognise one another. But if I take the law with Lassalle's stamp on
it, and therefore in his sense, I must also take it with his
justification for it. And what is that? As Lange showed shortly after
Lassalle's death: the Malthusian theory of population (preached by Lange
himself). But if this theory is correct, then once again I _cannot_
abolish the law, even if I abolish wage labour a hundred times over,
because the law then governs not only the system of wage labour but
_every_ social system. Basing themselves precisely on this, the
economists have been proving for fifty years and more that socialism
cannot abolish poverty _rooted in nature_, but can only _make it
general_, spread it evenly over the whole surface of society!

But all this is not the main point. _Quite apart_ from the _false_
Lassallean version of the law, the truly outrageous step backwards
consists in this:

Since Lassalle's death the scientific insight has made its way in _our_
party that _wages_ are not what they _appear_ to be, namely the _value_
or _price of labour_, but only a disguised form of the _value_ or _price
of labour-power_. With this, the whole bourgeois conception of wages
until then, as well as all the criticism directed against it until then,
was thrown overboard once and for all, and it was made clear that the
wage worker has permission to work for his own life, that is, _to live_,
only in so far as he works for a certain time for nothing for the
capitalist (and hence also for those who share in consuming the surplus
value along with him); that the whole capitalist system of production
turns on prolonging this unpaid labour, by extending the working day or
by developing productivity, by putting greater strain on labour-power,
etc.; that the system of wage labour is therefore a system of slavery,
and indeed of a slavery which becomes harsher in proportion as the
social productive forces of labour develop, whether the worker receives
better or worse payment. And after this insight has made more and more
headway in our party, there is a return to Lassalle's dogmas, although
people must by now have known that Lassalle _did not know_ what wages
were, but, following in the wake of the bourgeois economists, took the
appearance for the essence of the thing. It is as if, among slaves who
had at last got behind the secret of slavery and broken out in
rebellion, a slave still caught up in obsolete notions were to write on
the programme of the rebellion: Slavery must be abolished because the
feeding of slaves in the system of slavery cannot exceed a certain low
maximum! Does not the mere fact that the representatives of our party
were capable of so monstrous an attack on the insight that has spread
among the mass of the party prove by itself with what criminal levity,
with what lack of conscience, they went to work in drawing up the
compromise programme!

Instead of the vague closing phrase of the paragraph, “the elimination
of all social and political inequality”, it should have been said that
with the abolition of class distinctions all the social and political
inequality arising from them disappears of itself.

III

    “In order to _pave the way for the solution of the social
    question_, the German workers' party demands the establishment of
    producers' co-operatives with _state aid under the democratic
    control of the working people_. The producers' co-operatives _are
    to be called into being_ for industry and agriculture on such a
    scale _that the socialist organisation of the total labour will
    arise from them_.”

After the Lassallean “iron law of wages”, the prophet's remedy! The way
to it is “paved” in a worthy manner! In place of the class struggle that
actually exists there steps a newspaper scribbler's phrase — “_the_
social question”, for whose “_solution_” the way is being “paved”.
Instead of arising from the revolutionary process of transformation of
society, the “socialist organisation of the total labour” “arises” from
the “state aid” that the state gives to producers' co-operatives which
_it_, not the worker, “_calls into being_”. This is worthy of Lassalle's
fancy that one can build a new society with state loans just as one can
build a new railway!

From a remnant of shame, “state aid” is placed “under the democratic
control of the working people”.

In the first place, the majority of the “working people” in Germany
consists of peasants and not of proletarians.

Secondly, “democratic” in plain German means “ruled by the people”. But
what is “the control of the working people, ruled by the people”
supposed to mean? And above all in the case of a working people which,
through these very demands that it puts to the state, declares its full
awareness that it is neither in power nor ripe for power!

It is superfluous to go here into a critique of the recipe prescribed by
Buchez in the reign of Louis Philippe, _in opposition to_ the French
socialists, and taken up by the reactionary workers of the _Atelier_.
Nor does the chief offence lie in having written this specific miracle
cure into the programme, but in going back altogether from the
standpoint of a class movement to that of a sectarian movement.

That the workers want to establish the conditions for co-operative
production on a social scale, and to begin with in their own country,
that is, on a national scale, means only that they are working to
overthrow the present conditions of production, and it has nothing in
common with founding co-operative societies with state aid! As for the
present co-operative societies, they are of value _only_ in so far as
they are the independent creations of the workers, protected neither by
the governments nor by the bourgeois.

IV

I come now to the democratic section.

A. The free basis of the state

To begin with, according to II, the German workers' party strives for
“the free state”.

Free state — what is that?

It is by no means the aim of workers who have rid themselves of the
narrow mentality of humble subjects to make the state “free”. In the
German Empire the “state” is almost as “free” as in Russia. Freedom
consists in converting the state from an organ standing above society
into one completely subordinate to it, and today, too, the forms of the
state are more free or less free to the extent that they restrict the
“freedom of the state”.

The German workers' party — at least if it adopts this programme as its
own — shows that its socialist ideas do not go even skin-deep, in that,
instead of treating existing society (and the same holds for any future
one) as the _basis_ of the existing _state_ (or of the future state, for
future society), it treats the state rather as an independent entity
possessing its own “_intellectual, moral and free foundations_”.

And then there is the gross misuse the programme makes of the words
“_present-day state_” and “_present-day society_”, and the still grosser
misunderstanding it creates about the state to which it addresses its
demands!

“Present-day society” is capitalist society, which exists in all
civilised countries, more or less free of medieval admixture, more or
less modified by the particular historical development of each country,
more or less developed. The “present-day state”, on the other hand,
changes with each country's frontier. It is different in the
Prusso-German Empire from what it is in Switzerland, different in
England from what it is in the United States. “_The_ present-day state”
is therefore a fiction.

Nevertheless, the different states of the different civilised countries,
in spite of the motley variety of their forms, all have this in common:
they stand on the ground of modern bourgeois society, only one more and
another less developed in the capitalist sense. They therefore also have
certain essential features in common. In this sense one can speak of the
“present-day state system”, in contrast to the future, in which its
present root, bourgeois society, will have died away.

The question then arises: what transformation will the state system
undergo in a communist society? In other words, what social functions
will remain there that are analogous to the present functions of the
state? This question can only be answered scientifically, and one gets
not a flea's hop nearer to the problem by combining the word “people”
with the word “state” a thousand times over.

Between capitalist and communist society lies the period of the
revolutionary transformation of the one into the other. To this there
corresponds also a political transition period, whose state can be
nothing other than the _revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat_.

Now the programme has nothing to do either with the latter or with the
future state system of communist society.

Its political demands contain nothing beyond the old democratic litany
known to the whole world: universal suffrage, direct legislation,
popular rights, a people's militia, etc. They are a mere echo of the
bourgeois People's Party, of the League of Peace and Freedom. They are
all demands which, in so far as they are not exaggerated into fantastic
notions, have already been _realised_. Only the state to which they
belong lies not within the borders of the German Empire but in
Switzerland, the United States, etc. This sort of “state of the future”
is a _present-day state_, although it exists outside “the framework” of
the German Empire.

But one thing has been forgotten. Since the German workers' party
expressly declares that it is operating within “the present-day national
state”, that is, within its own state, the Prusso-German Empire —
otherwise most of its demands would be meaningless, since one demands
only what one does not yet have — it ought not to have forgotten the
chief thing, namely that all those pretty little things rest on the
recognition of the so-called sovereignty of the people, and are
therefore in place only in a _democratic republic_.

Since one has not the courage — and wisely so, for the circumstances
call for caution — to demand the democratic republic, as the French
workers' programmes did under Louis Philippe and under Louis Napoleon,
one should not have resorted to the subterfuge, neither “honest” nor
dignified, of demanding things that make sense only in a democratic
republic from a state which is nothing but a military despotism, trimmed
with parliamentary forms, mixed with a feudal admixture, already
influenced by the bourgeoisie, bureaucratically carpentered together and
guarded by the police, and of assuring this state into the bargain that
one imagines one can force such things on it “by legal means”!

Even vulgar democracy, which sees the millennium in the democratic
republic and has no inkling that it is precisely in this last form of
state of bourgeois society that the class struggle has to be fought out
to a finish — even it towers mountains high above this kind of
democratism, which keeps within the bounds of what the police allow and
logic does not.

That by “state” is in fact meant the government machine, or the state in
so far as it forms a special organism of its own, separated from society
by the division of labour, is shown by the very words: “The German
workers' party demands _as the economic basis of the state_: a single
progressive income tax, etc.” Taxes are the economic basis of the
government machinery and of nothing else. In the state of the future
that exists in Switzerland, this demand has been pretty well fulfilled.
Income tax presupposes the different sources of income of the different
social classes, and hence capitalist society. It is therefore nothing
remarkable that the Liverpool Financial Reformers — bourgeois headed by
Gladstone's brother — are putting forward the same demand as the
programme.

B. The intellectual and moral basis of the state

    “The German workers' party demands as the intellectual and moral
    basis of the state: 1. Universal and _equal popular education_ by
    the state. Universal compulsory school attendance. Free
    instruction.”

_Equal popular education?_

What do people imagine is meant by these words? Is it believed that in
present-day society (and it is only with present-day society that one is
dealing) education can be _equal_ for all classes? Or is it demanded
that the upper classes too shall be compulsorily reduced to the modicum
of education — the elementary school — that alone is compatible with the
economic conditions not only of the wage workers but of the peasants as
well?

“Universal compulsory school attendance. Free instruction.” The former
exists in Germany, the latter in Switzerland and the United States, as
far as elementary schools are concerned. If in some states of the latter
“higher” educational establishments are also “free”, that in fact only
means meeting the cost of the education of the upper classes out of the
general tax fund. Incidentally, the same holds for the “free
administration of justice” demanded under A, 5. Criminal justice is to
be had free everywhere; civil justice turns almost exclusively on
conflicts over property and so concerns almost exclusively the
propertied classes. Are they to conduct their lawsuits at the expense of
the national purse?

The paragraph on the schools should at least have demanded technical
schools (theoretical and practical) in combination with the elementary
school.

Entirely objectionable is “_popular education by the state_”. Laying
down by a general law the funds of the elementary schools, the
qualifications of the teaching staff, the branches of instruction, etc.,
and, as happens in the United States, supervising the fulfilment of
these legal requirements by means of state inspectors, is something
quite different from appointing the state as educator of the people!
Government and church should rather be excluded equally from any
influence on the school. In the Prusso-German Empire above all (and let
no one get out of it with the lame excuse that one is speaking of a
“state of the future”; we have seen how matters stand with that), it is,
on the contrary, the state that needs a very rough education by the
people.

But the whole programme, for all its democratic jingle, is tainted
through and through by the Lassallean sect's servile belief in the
state, or, what is no better, by a democratic faith in miracles; or
rather it is a compromise between these two kinds of faith in miracles,
both equally remote from socialism.

“_Freedom of science_”, runs a clause of the Prussian constitution. Why,
then, have it here?

“_Freedom of conscience_”! If, at this time of the Kulturkampf, one
wanted to remind liberalism of its old catchwords, it could surely only
have been done in this form: Everyone must be able to relieve his
religious needs, like his bodily ones, without the police poking their
noses in. But the workers' party ought at any rate to have taken this
opportunity to express its awareness that bourgeois “freedom of
conscience” is nothing but the toleration of every possible kind of
_religious freedom of conscience_, and that for its part it seeks rather
to free consciences from the spectre of religion. But it chooses not to
go beyond the “bourgeois” level.

I have now come to the end, for the appendix that follows in the
programme is not a _characteristic_ part of it. I can therefore be very
brief here.

“_2. Normal working day._”

In no other country has a workers' party confined itself to so vague a
demand; they have always fixed the length of the working day that they
regard as normal under the given circumstances.

“3. Restriction of women's labour and prohibition of child labour.”

The standardisation of the working day must already include the
restriction of women's labour, in so far as this relates to the length,
breaks, etc., of the working day; otherwise it can only mean the
exclusion of women from branches of industry that are especially
unhealthy for the female body or morally objectionable for the female
sex. If that is what was meant, it should have been said.

“_Prohibition of child labour_”!

Here it was absolutely necessary to state the _age limit_.

A _general prohibition_ of child labour is incompatible with the
existence of large-scale industry and is therefore an empty pious wish.

To carry it out — if it were possible — would be reactionary, since,
with strict regulation of working time according to the different age
groups and other precautionary measures for the protection of children,
the early combination of productive labour with education is one of the
most powerful means of transforming present-day society.

“4. State supervision of factory, workshop and domestic industry.”

In relation to the Prusso-German state, it should definitely have been
demanded that the inspectors be removable only by a court of law; that
any worker be able to denounce them to the courts for neglect of duty;
and that they must belong to the medical profession.

“5. Regulation of prison labour.”

A petty demand in a general workers' programme. In any case, it should
have been stated clearly that the workers have no wish, out of fear of
competition, to see ordinary criminals treated like cattle, and above
all no wish to cut them off from their only means of reform, productive
labour. That was surely the least one might have expected from
socialists.

“6. An effective liability law.”

It should have been said what is meant by an “effective” liability law.

It may be noted in passing that, in speaking of the normal working day,
the programme has overlooked the part of factory legislation that
concerns health measures and safeguards against danger, etc. The
liability law only comes into operation once these regulations have been
violated.

In short, this appendix too is distinguished by slipshod drafting.

_Dixi et salvavi animam meam._ (I have spoken and saved my soul.)
